      Experiment two · trying to beat 93

      Can 18 fences make 94 yards?

      We can draw 93. Whether 94 is possible remains open. A checked computer proof rules out 94 for drawings unchanged by a one-third turn, where every pair of lines crosses and no three meet at a point. Read the result and its limits.

      Research details: the gap, the searches, and their limits

      The crossing-pattern search also found a different straight-line drawing with 93 yards. The unrestricted searches for 94, and a control asking for 93, gave no answer in two hours. Those timeouts establish no impossibility.

      The gap between 76 and 93

      Our original search used three random seeds and made 1,200 proposals per seed, usually changing one line. Each seed began with a small-coefficient drawing and restarted from a new one every 200 proposals. Its best drawing had 76 triangular yards. Bader’s earlier construction uses a threefold pattern and makes 93. An exact audit finds 58 bounded fence segments that do not border a yard in our 76 drawing and only three in the rebuilt published drawing. The count describes two drawings, not a method for moving between them.

      Several distinct fences may share one corner. A fan of 17 fences through one point, crossed by an eighteenth fence, traces 136 triangle outlines but forms only 16 undivided yards. The larger outlines contain fences inside them. Read the research notes for the exact audit, Savchuk’s crossing-order method and our search of every crossing pattern. That search proved that no drawing with the threefold symmetry of Bader’s construction reaches 94 yards when every pair of fences crosses and no three share a corner. Finding 94 would require an exact drawing. Proving 93 is the maximum would require ruling out every legal 94-yard drawing.

      The table below records four earlier runs near Bader’s drawing. This site retains their summaries, not their per-change logs.

      How the search chose its moves

      We used Python’s seeded random generator, with seeds 17 and 42 for each of two methods. One changed a fence’s position. The other could change its direction or position. Each change adjusted one whole-number coefficient in the line equation ax + by + c = 0. Its size was the larger of 1 and floor(max(|a|, |b|) / d), with d chosen from 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 and either sign chosen at random.

      The recorded method kept ordinary moves when the count stayed level or rose. Every 250 proposals, it restarted from Bader’s construction and applied three changes, accepting a valid restart even if its count fell. Invalid drawings were rejected. Both exact counters had to agree. Each run allowed 1,500 proposals or 120 seconds, whichever came first. All four summaries report that they used their proposal budget. These settings describe the reported 6,000-change experiment, not the earlier search that found 76.

      The result across all 20,736 rule tables

      The six-step robot shows a halt with four marked squares. Our bounded run tested all 20,736 two-state rule sets for at most 100 steps, and a third simulator agreed on those bounded outcomes. For 5,912 machines, it found neither a halt nor a repeat proof by step 100, so they are unresolved by our checker at that cutoff. A separate certificate check in the source repository proves that each of them never halts, and it certifies none of the machines that do halt. Together these confirm the established result S(2) = 6 (also listed by MathWorld) for this standard blank-tape, two-state, two-symbol model: every halting machine stops within six steps. These checks reproduce the established result.

      Reading the evidence

      Each kind of evidence proves one thing.

      01

      Witness

      A checked drawing with 93 yards proves that 93 is possible. It says nothing about whether 94 is.

      02

      Proof

      Proving a maximum would mean showing that no drawing can do better. Checking individual drawings does not settle that question.

      03

      Novelty

      Bader published the 93-yard drawing before we rebuilt it, so our version is a reconstruction of prior art.

      Busy Beaver lesson

      Three outcomes, three meanings

      A halting robot shows that its step count is possible. When it returns to an earlier state with the same 1s at the same distances from it, it will never halt. Reaching step 100 without either result leaves it unresolved by this checker. The standard two-state result settles nonhalting only when applied separately to this exact machine model.
